**Script**

- **AMARA:** A cross-border transfer is a chain of payment orders.
- **AMARA:** The buyer, called the originator, instructs its own bank.
- **AMARA:** That bank sends a payment order, usually by a secure message network like SWIFT, often through an intermediary bank.
- **AMARA:** Finally, the beneficiary's bank credits SafariTech, the beneficiary.
- **AMARA:** Within Africa, regional systems help too, like the East African Payment System, and the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System.
- **NIA:** So each link in the chain is a separate payment order, from a sender to a receiving bank.

## Scene 3: The Model Law on credit transfers `1:07`

*Teaching board · Syllabus C1(c)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Model Law on Credit Transfers (1992)
- Sender pays on acceptance
- Execute, normally the same day
- Money-back guarantee, with interest
- Interest for delay
- Complete when beneficiary's bank accepts
- Caption: Sending and receiving banks in different states

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** UNCITRAL's Model Law on International Credit Transfers, from 1992, applies where a sending bank and its receiving bank are in different states.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A sender must pay the receiving bank once that bank accepts its payment order.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A receiving bank that accepts must execute the order, normally on the day it is received.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** If the transfer is not completed, there is a money-back guarantee. The originator's bank must refund the originator, with interest.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A bank that delays must pay interest for the delay.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And the transfer is completed when the beneficiary's bank accepts the payment order for the beneficiary. That is when the debt is paid.
- **KITO:** So once my bank in Dar accepts the order, the buyer has paid me, even before I see the money!

## Scene 4: A letter of credit, step by step `2:01`

*Animated diagram · Studio · Syllabus C1(e)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Documentary letter of credit
- Nodes: Buyer (applicant); Issuing bank (Dubai); Advising bank (Nairobi); Seller (beneficiary)
- Arrows: B → IB (1 apply); IB → AB (2 credit opened); AB → S (advise); S → AB (3 documents); AB → IB (4 documents); IB → B (5 documents)
- Caption: Seller paid

**Script**

- **AMARA:** For a new buyer, a documentary letter of credit is the classic answer. The seller gets paid promptly, and the buyer can still get credit.
- **AMARA:** One. The buyer asks its bank, the issuing bank, to open a credit in favour of SafariTech.
- **AMARA:** Two. The issuing bank undertakes to pay against the stated documents, and asks a bank in the seller's country, the advising bank, to notify the seller.
- **AMARA:** Three. SafariTech ships, and presents the documents, like the bill of lading and invoice, to the advising bank.
- **AMARA:** Four. The advising bank checks the documents, pays SafariTech, and sends them on to the issuing bank.
- **AMARA:** Five. The issuing bank checks them, reimburses the advising bank, debits the buyer, and releases the documents, so the buyer can collect the goods.
- **NIA:** So nobody hands over goods or money until the paperwork is right.

## Scene 5: Rules of the letter of credit `2:52`

*Teaching board · Syllabus C1(e)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Letter of credit principles
- Autonomy — Separate from the sale · documents, not goods
- Strict compliance — Documents must match exactly
- Irrevocable — No change without the seller's consent
- Confirmed vs unconfirmed — Second bank's promise, or issuing bank only
- Fraud exception — Known fraud: bank need not pay
- Caption: ICC Uniform Customs and Practice: UCP 600

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Most letters of credit follow the ICC's Uniform Customs and Practice, UCP 600.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Autonomy. The credit is separate from the sale contract. Banks deal in documents, not goods.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Strict compliance. The documents must match the credit's terms on their face. A small mismatch can mean no payment.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Irrevocable. The buyer cannot change or cancel the credit without the seller's agreement. Under UCP 600, every credit is irrevocable.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Confirmed. If the advising bank adds its own promise to pay, the seller has two banks behind it. Unconfirmed, it relies on the issuing bank alone.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And there is a narrow fraud exception. A bank need not pay a beneficiary who presents documents it knows are fraudulent.
- **AMARA:** For an unknown buyer, I'd always advise a confirmed credit.

## Scene 6: Letters of comfort `3:50`

*Case file · Bank (Nairobi) · Syllabus C1(e)*

**On screen**

- **Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining Corp** (Court of Appeal, England · 1989)
  - Facts: Parent's letter: our policy is to keep the subsidiary able to pay
  - Held: A statement of present policy, not a contractual promise
  - Lesson: Want liability? Take a guarantee
  - Stamp: NOT BINDING

**Script**

- **AMARA:** Sometimes a lender to a subsidiary only gets a letter of comfort from the parent company.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A letter of comfort usually says the parent intends to support its subsidiary. But it is normally a statement of present policy, not a binding promise.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** In Kleinwort Benson against Malaysia Mining Corporation, the parent wrote that its policy was to ensure its subsidiary was always in a position to meet its liabilities.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** When the subsidiary collapsed, the bank sued the parent. The Court of Appeal held the letter was only a statement of policy, not a contractual promise. The bank lost.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** So if you need the parent to be liable, ask for a guarantee, not comfort.
