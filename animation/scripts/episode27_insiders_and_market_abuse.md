## Scene 2: Three offences

*Teaching board · Syllabus H1(a)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Insider dealing offences
- Dealing — Buying or selling price-affected securities
- Encouraging — Even if no one deals
- Disclosing — Outside the proper course of a job

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** In the UK model, the Criminal Justice Act of 1993 creates three insider dealing offences.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Dealing: an insider buys or sells price-affected securities, on a regulated market or through a professional intermediary.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Encouraging: an insider encourages someone else to deal, whether or not they actually do.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Disclosing: an insider passes on the information, other than in the proper performance of their job.
- **THE AUDITOR:** So Kito telling Hans could be disclosing, and Hans buying could be dealing.

## Scene 3: What is inside information?

*Teaching board · Syllabus H1(a)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Inside information
- About particular securities or issuers
- Specific or precise
- Not made public
- Likely to significantly move the price

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Inside information has four features.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It relates to particular securities, or particular issuers.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It is specific or precise, not just a vague rumour.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It has not been made public.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And if it were made public, it would be likely to have a significant effect on the price.
- **NIA:** A confirmed government contract, not yet announced, that will move the share price. That ticks all four boxes.

## Scene 4: Who is an insider?

*Animated diagram · Studio · Syllabus H1(a)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** The chain of insiders
- Nodes: Kilima's finance director; Kito; Hans
- Arrows: fd → k (tip); k → h (tip)

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** An insider has inside information, and knows it is inside information from an inside source.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Primary insiders get it by being a director, employee or shareholder of an issuer, or through their job, office or profession, like an auditor or lawyer.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Secondary insiders, sometimes called tippees, get it directly or indirectly from a primary insider.
- **THE AUDITOR:** So the finance director is a primary insider, and Kito and Hans became secondary insiders the moment they heard.

## Scene 5: Defences and penalties

*Teaching board · Syllabus H1(a)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Defences and penalties
- No expected profit from the information
- Reasonably believed widely disclosed
- Would have dealt anyway — + market maker and stabilisation defences
- Crime: prison and/or unlimited fine — The contract itself is not void
- Pop-up card **Local lens: East Africa**: Tanzania: Capital Markets and Securities Act · Kenya: Capital Markets Act · both ban insider dealing

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** There are defences. The accused did not expect to profit because the information was price-sensitive.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Or believed, on reasonable grounds, that it had been widely disclosed.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Or would have done the same thing anyway, without the information. There are also special defences for market makers and price stabilisation.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Insider dealing is a crime, punishable by imprisonment, an unlimited fine, or both. Oddly, the contract itself is not made void.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And it's not just a UK idea. Tanzania's Capital Markets and Securities Act, and Kenya's Capital Markets Act, also ban insider dealing, policed by their capital markets regulators.
- **HANS:** Then I'll wait for the public announcement. Keine Aktien heute. No shares today.

## Scene 6: Market abuse

*Side-by-side comparison · Studio · Syllabus H1(b)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Market abuse: civil and criminal
- **Civil regime (UK MAR):** Insider dealing; Unlawful disclosure; Market manipulation; Balance of probabilities; Fines · censure · injunctions · restitution
- **Criminal offences:** Misleading statements; Misleading impressions; Benchmark manipulation; Beyond reasonable doubt

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Market abuse is wider, and in the UK it is mainly a civil regime, under the Market Abuse Regulation, enforced by the Financial Conduct Authority.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It covers three behaviours: insider dealing, unlawful disclosure of inside information, and market manipulation.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Because it's civil, the regulator need only prove it on the balance of probabilities, and intent isn't always needed. Sanctions include unlimited fines, public censure, injunctions and restitution.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Separately, it is a crime to make misleading statements, or create a misleading impression, to induce people to invest, or to manipulate benchmarks.
- **NIA:** So the regulator has two weapons: civil penalties, and criminal prosecution.

## Scene 7: What manipulation looks like

*Teaching board · Syllabus H1(b)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Market manipulation
- False or misleading signals
- Wash trades
- Pump and dump
- False rumours
- Artificial price
- Online hype counts too

**Script**

- **THE AUDITOR:** Market manipulation means distorting the market. Watch for these patterns.
- **THE AUDITOR:** Trades that give false or misleading signals about supply, demand or price.
- **THE AUDITOR:** Wash trades, where someone trades with themselves to fake activity.
- **THE AUDITOR:** Pump and dump: spreading hype to push a price up, then selling.
- **THE AUDITOR:** Spreading false or misleading information or rumours, including online.
- **THE AUDITOR:** And securing an artificial price, for example by cornering the supply.
- **KITO:** So even a social media post hyping Kilima shares could be market abuse!
