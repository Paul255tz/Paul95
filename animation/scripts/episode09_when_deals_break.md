## Scene 2: Fundamental breach `0:32`

*Side-by-side comparison · Studio · Syllabus B2(c)(i)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Article 25: fundamental breach
- **Fundamental:** Substantial deprivation of what was expected; Result was foreseeable; Innocent party may avoid at once
- **Not fundamental:** Damages, price reduction, repair; Fix an extra period first; Small delay or minor defect

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A breach is fundamental if it causes such detriment that it substantially deprives the other party of what it was entitled to expect.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Unless the party in breach did not foresee that result, and a reasonable person in the same position would not have foreseen it either.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Why does it matter? Because only a fundamental breach lets the innocent party avoid the contract straight away. Otherwise, it claims damages, or first fixes an extra period.
- **NIA:** So a small delay, or a minor defect, won't end the contract on its own.

## Scene 3: Anticipatory breach `1:11`

*Timeline · Studio · Syllabus B2(c)(i)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Anticipatory breach (Arts 71–72)
- Warning signs — Ability, credit or conduct
- Suspend + notify — Resume if adequate assurance
- Stop in transit — Even if buyer holds documents
- Avoid in advance — Clear fundamental breach ahead

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Sometimes trouble is visible before performance is even due. That's anticipatory breach.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** If it becomes apparent that the other side will not perform a substantial part of its obligations, because of a serious deficiency in its ability, or its creditworthiness, or its conduct, a party may suspend its own performance.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It must give notice of the suspension at once, and must carry on if the other side gives adequate assurance.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A seller who has already shipped can stop the goods reaching the buyer, even if the buyer holds the documents.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And if it is clear that a fundamental breach will happen, the party may avoid the contract in advance, giving reasonable notice so the other side can offer assurance.
- **HANS:** So I may pause shipments, but I must tell Kito, and restart if his bank guarantees payment.
- **AMARA:** Our bank can issue that guarantee today.

## Scene 4: Instalment contracts `2:11`

*Teaching board · Syllabus B2(c)(i)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Instalment contracts (Art 73)
- Avoid for that instalment — If that instalment is a fundamental breach
- Avoid for future instalments — Good grounds to expect future fundamental breach
- Avoid for past instalments too — If deliveries are interdependent

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** With ten monthly deliveries, each instalment is judged separately.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** If one instalment is a fundamental breach, the buyer may avoid the contract for that instalment.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** If it gives good grounds to expect fundamental breaches in future instalments, the buyer may avoid for the future too, within a reasonable time.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And if the deliveries depend on each other, so earlier ones are useless alone, the buyer may avoid for past ones as well.
- **NIA:** So if the first delivery is badly defective, the whole deal may go. But if seven are fine and the eighth is faulty, we probably just reject the eighth, and claim damages.

## Scene 5: Damages `2:54`

*Teaching board · Syllabus B2(c)(ii)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Damages (Arts 74–77)
- Loss caused, including lost profit
- Limited to foreseeable loss
- Substitute deal: price difference
- No substitute: market price difference
- Duty to mitigate

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Damages equal the loss, including lost profit, caused by the breach.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** But only loss the party in breach foresaw, or ought to have foreseen, as a possible consequence when the contract was made.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** If the contract is avoided and the innocent party makes a substitute deal, it can claim the difference between the contract price and the substitute price.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** With no substitute deal, it can claim the difference from the current market price.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And it must mitigate. Damages are reduced by any loss it could reasonably have avoided.
- **KITO:** So if Hans stops supplying, I buy parts elsewhere, at a sensible price, and claim the extra cost.

## Scene 7: Exemptions: beyond control `4:03`

*Teaching board · Syllabus B2(c)(iv)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Exemption (Art 79)
- Impediment beyond its control
- Not reasonably foreseeable at contract
- Could not avoid or overcome it
- Notice within a reasonable time
- Pop-up card **Third party caused it?**: Exempt only if BOTH the party and the third party would be exempt
- Pop-up card **Limits**: Only while the impediment lasts · blocks damages only · other remedies survive

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Now Hans's flood. A party is not liable for a failure if it proves four things.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The failure was due to an impediment beyond its control.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It could not reasonably have taken it into account when contracting.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It could not reasonably have avoided or overcome it, or its consequences.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And it gave notice of the impediment within a reasonable time.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** If a third party, like Hans's supplier, caused the failure, Hans is exempt only if both he and the supplier would be exempt.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The exemption lasts only while the impediment lasts. And it only blocks damages. The other remedies, like avoidance, remain available.
- **HANS:** So I can't be sued for damages because of the flood, but Kito could still end the contract if the delay is fundamental.
