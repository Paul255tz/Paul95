## Scene 2: Four bribery offences

*Teaching board · Syllabus H1(d)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Bribery Act 2010
- Bribing another person — Offer, promise, give
- Being bribed — Request, agree, accept
- Bribing a foreign public official
- Failure to prevent bribery — Commercial organisations · associated persons
- Caption: Up to 10 years · unlimited fines · no facilitation payments

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The Bribery Act has four offences.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Bribing: offering, promising or giving a financial or other advantage, to induce or reward improper performance of a function.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Being bribed: requesting, agreeing to receive, or accepting such an advantage.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Bribing a foreign public official, to obtain or keep business.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And a corporate offence: a commercial organisation fails to prevent bribery by an associated person, such as an employee, agent or subsidiary, acting for its benefit.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Individuals face up to ten years in prison. Companies face unlimited fines. Facilitation payments, like Kito's gift, are not exempt. Reasonable, proportionate hospitality is fine.

## Scene 4: Failure to prevent tax evasion

*Animated diagram · Studio · Syllabus H1(e)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Failure to prevent facilitation of tax evasion
- Nodes: 1. Taxpayer evades tax; 2. Associated person facilitates; 3. Body failed to prevent
- Arrows: t → a; a → b
- Pop-up card **Defence: reasonable prevention procedures**: UK and foreign tax · relevant body = company or partnership · unlimited fine

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The Criminal Finances Act 2017 made a relevant body, meaning a company or a partnership, liable for failing to prevent the criminal facilitation of tax evasion.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It works in three stages.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** One: a taxpayer criminally evades tax.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Two: a person associated with the body, like an employee or agent, criminally facilitates that evasion, while acting for the body.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Three: the body failed to prevent it.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It covers UK tax, and foreign tax where there's a UK connection. The defence is reasonable prevention procedures. The penalty, an unlimited fine.
- **THE AUDITOR:** This one matters for accountancy firms. One staff member helping a client hide income could make the whole firm liable.

## Scene 5: Failure to prevent fraud

*Teaching board · Syllabus H1(e)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Failure to prevent fraud (2025)
- Large organisations only — 2 of: 250+ staff · £36m turnover · £18m assets
- Associated person commits fraud — Intending to benefit the organisation
- Defence: reasonable procedures — Penalty: unlimited fine
- Caption: In force 1 September 2025 (ECCTA 2023)
- Caption: Senior managers' crimes attributed to the company

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The newest offence came into force on the first of September 2025, under the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It applies to large organisations, meeting two of three tests: more than two hundred and fifty employees, turnover over thirty-six million pounds, or assets over eighteen million.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The organisation is liable if an associated person commits a fraud offence, such as false representation or false accounting, intending to benefit the organisation.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The defence: reasonable fraud prevention procedures. The penalty: an unlimited fine.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The same Act also makes it easier to convict companies, by attributing the economic crimes of senior managers to the company.

## Scene 6: Fraudulent or wrongful trading?

*Side-by-side comparison · Studio · Syllabus H1(f)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Fraudulent vs wrongful trading
- **Fraudulent:** Intent to defraud; Real dishonesty; Crime (Companies Act): up to 10 years; Civil in liquidation: anyone knowingly party
- **Wrongful:** No dishonesty needed; Knew or ought to have concluded: no way out; Civil only: directors contribute; Defence: every step to minimise loss

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Two more ways directors can be made to pay.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Fraudulent trading: carrying on business with intent to defraud creditors, or for any fraudulent purpose. It needs real dishonesty.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It is a crime under the Companies Act, whether or not the company is wound up, with up to ten years in prison. And in a liquidation, the court can order anyone knowingly involved, not just directors, to contribute to the assets.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Wrongful trading needs no dishonesty. A director is liable if they knew, or ought to have concluded, that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation or administration, and carried on.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It is civil only. The court can order the director to contribute. The defence: they took every step to minimise the loss to creditors.
- **THE AUDITOR:** The essential difference is intent to defraud.

## Scene 7: Wrongful trading in court

*Case file · Courtroom · Syllabus H1(e)–(f)*

**On screen**

- **Re Produce Marketing Consortium Ltd** (High Court, England · 1989)
  - Facts: Directors kept trading long after insolvency was clear
  - Held: Liable to contribute · objective + subjective test
  - Also: False representation · false accounting · phoenix names
  - Stamp: DIRECTORS PAY

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** In Re Produce Marketing Consortium, two directors kept a fruit importing company trading for over a year after they should have seen that insolvent liquidation was inevitable.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** They were ordered to contribute to the company's assets. The court judged them by what a reasonably diligent director, with their own knowledge and experience, would have concluded.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Other crimes around company life include fraud by false representation, false accounting, concealing assets before a winding up, and reusing a failed company's name. And of course, disqualification.
