## Scene 2: Market, planned and mixed `0:34`

*Side-by-side comparison · Studio · Syllabus A1(a)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** How an economy allocates resources
- **Market:** Supply and demand set prices; Wealth held privately
- **Planned:** State allocates resources; Prices fixed centrally; Tanzania: Ujamaa after the 1967 Arusha Declaration
- **Mixed:** Markets set most prices; Taxes, subsidies, regulation; Tanzania and Kenya today

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** In a market economy, supply and demand set prices, and most wealth is held privately.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** In a planned economy, the state allocates resources and fixes prices.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Tanzania tried this after the Arusha Declaration of 1967, with the Ujamaa policy of state ownership.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A mixed economy combines both. Markets set most prices, but government steps in with taxes, subsidies and regulation.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Since the reforms of the 1980s and 90s, Tanzania and Kenya are both mixed economies.
- **NIA:** And the law has to fit whichever system a country chooses.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Exactly. Politics decides who makes the law. The law then protects property and enforces contracts, which is what investors look for.

## Scene 3: The common law family `1:30`

*Teaching board · Syllabus A1(d)(i)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Common law
- Born in England after 1066 — King's judges unify local customs
- Judicial precedent — Follow the reasoning of higher courts
- Equity fills the gaps — Injunctions, specific performance, rescission
- Judicature Acts 1873–75 — Courts merged · equity prevails in a conflict
- Received in Tanzania and Kenya — Common law + local statutes + customary law

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** First family: common law. It began in England after 1066, when the king's judges made local customs common to the whole country.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Its cornerstone is judicial precedent. Judges follow the reasoning of earlier, higher courts.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** When money damages were not enough, equity grew up beside it, with remedies like injunctions and specific performance.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The Judicature Acts of the 1870s merged the courts. Where common law and equity conflict, equity prevails.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Through colonial history, the common law was received into East Africa. Tanzania and Kenya still use it today, alongside their own statutes and customary law.
- **KITO:** So our judges in Dar es Salaam follow precedent too!

## Scene 4: How precedent binds `2:25`

*Side-by-side comparison · Studio · Syllabus A1(d)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Judicial precedent
- **Binding when…:** It is a point of law; It is part of the ratio decidendi (obiter is only persuasive); Material facts are similar; It comes from a higher court
- **Avoided when…:** Facts are distinguished; Made per incuriam (without care); Too wide or too obscure; Since overruled

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A precedent binds a later court only when four things are true.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It is a point of law, not a point of fact.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** It is part of the ratio decidendi, the reason for the decision.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The material facts are sufficiently similar.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And the earlier court was higher in the court hierarchy.
- **NIA:** And when can a judge avoid it?
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** By distinguishing the facts, or if the earlier ruling was made per incuriam, meaning carelessly, or was too wide or too obscure, or has since been overruled.

## Scene 5: The civil law family `3:01`

*Side-by-side comparison · Studio · Syllabus A1(d)(ii)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Common law vs civil law
- **Common law:** Built case by case; Precedent binds; Adversarial trial; Judge as referee
- **Civil law:** Roman roots, written codes; France 1804 · Germany 1900; Cases guide, don't bind; Inquisitorial trial; Judge as investigator

**Script**

- *SFX: whoosh*
- **HANS:** Guten Tag! In Germany, our judges start with the code, not with old cases.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** That is the second family: civil law. It grew from Roman law, and today it is written down in comprehensive codes.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** France's Civil Code of 1804, and Germany's Civil Code of 1900, are the famous examples.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Judges apply the code to the facts. Earlier decisions guide them, but they are not strictly binding.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Trials tend to be inquisitorial, with the judge leading the questioning, and legal scholars' writings carry real weight.
- **NIA:** So in common law the judge is more like a referee, but in civil law more like an investigator.

## Scene 6: The Sharia law family `3:50`

*Teaching board · Syllabus A1(d)(iii)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Sharia law
- Quran — The primary source
- Sunnah — Accepted practice of the Prophet
- Ijtihad and ijma — Independent reasoning · consensus of scholars
- Qiyas — Analogy: the nearest thing to precedent
- Schools of law — Hanafi · Maliki · Shafi'i · Hanbali (and Shia)
- Kadhi's courts: Kenya, Zanzibar — Muslim personal law: marriage, inheritance
- Pop-up card **Riba is prohibited**: Islamic finance shares profit and risk instead of charging interest

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The third family is Sharia law. The word means the path to a watering place.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Its primary source is the Quran.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Next comes the Sunnah, the accepted practice of the Prophet.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Where these are silent, scholars use ijtihad, independent reasoning. Ijma is the consensus of scholars.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And qiyas is reasoning by analogy. It is the nearest thing to judicial precedent.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** The schools of law, such as Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali, add detailed interpretation.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Closer to home, Kadhi's courts in Kenya and Zanzibar apply Islamic law to personal matters such as marriage and inheritance.
- **KITO:** And in business, interest is forbidden, right? That matters for our Dubai customers.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Right. Riba, or interest, is prohibited, so Islamic finance shares profit instead.

## Scene 8: Treaties, conventions and model laws `5:27`

*Teaching board · Syllabus A2(b)*

**On screen**

**Heading:** Tools of harmonisation
- Treaty / convention — Binds states that ratify · e.g. CISG
- Model law — Template states may adopt · e.g. arbitration
- Model code / principles — Guidance only · e.g. UNIDROIT Principles
- Uniform contract terms — Bind by agreement · e.g. Incoterms

**Script**

- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Harmonisation comes in different strengths.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A treaty or convention binds the states that ratify it, like the UN Sales Convention, which we'll meet in Episode 5.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** A model law is a template. Each state decides whether to adopt it, and can adapt it, like the UNCITRAL Model Law on arbitration.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** Model codes and principles, like the UNIDROIT Principles, guide parties and lawmakers without binding anyone.
- **PROFESSOR LEX:** And uniform trade terms like Incoterms bind only because the parties write them into their contract.
- **NIA:** So a treaty binds states, but a model law only binds a country that chooses to adopt it.
