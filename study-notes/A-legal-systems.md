# A. Essential elements of legal systems

**Episodes 1–4** · Syllabus A1 (business, political and legal systems), A2
(international trade, regulation and conflict of laws), A3 (alternative dispute
resolution).

## A1. Business, political and legal systems

### Economic, political and legal systems are linked (A1a)

| Economy | How resources are allocated | Prices | Who holds wealth |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Market** | Supply and demand | Set by the market | Mostly private |
| **Planned** | The state decides centrally | Fixed by the state | Mostly the state |
| **Mixed** | Markets, plus government action | Market, adjusted by taxes and subsidies | Shared |

- The political system decides **who makes the law and how**. The legal system
  then protects property, enforces contracts and resolves disputes, which is
  what investors and traders rely on.
- *Local lens:* Tanzania ran a planned economy under **Ujamaa** after the
  **Arusha Declaration (1967)**, then liberalised from the mid-1980s. Tanzania
  and Kenya are both mixed economies today.

### Separation of powers (A1b)

| Branch | Role | UK example |
|---|---|---|
| Legislature | Makes the law | Parliament |
| Executive | Puts the law into effect | Government and ministers |
| Judiciary | Resolves disputes and interprets the law | The courts |

The **rule of law** means everyone, government included, is subject to the
law. Separation of powers stops any one branch from becoming too powerful.
Kenya's Constitution of 2010 is a clear modern example: it separates the three
arms explicitly.

### Criminal and civil law (A1c)

| | Civil law | Criminal law |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Remedy for a private wrong (compensation) | Punish an offence against society |
| Parties | Claimant v defendant | Prosecution (the state) v accused |
| Standard of proof | Balance of probabilities | Beyond reasonable doubt |
| Outcome | Damages, or an equitable remedy | Fine, imprisonment, community order |

Classify the facts first. One event can lead to both a civil claim and a
prosecution.

### Legal systems (A1d)

**Common law** (England; received into Tanzania, Kenya and much of the
Commonwealth):

- It developed from 1066, as royal judges made local customs "common" to the
  whole country.
- Its cornerstone is **judicial precedent** (*stare decisis*). The **ratio
  decidendi** (the legal reason necessary for the decision) binds lower courts.
  **Obiter dicta** are only persuasive.
- A precedent binds when four things are true: it is a point of law, it is part
  of the ratio, the material facts are sufficiently similar, and the earlier
  court is higher in the hierarchy.
- Ways to avoid a precedent: **distinguishing** (different material facts),
  **overruling** (a higher court changes the rule in a later case),
  **reversing** (the same case on appeal), a decision made *per incuriam*
  (carelessly), or a ratio that is too wide or obscure.
- **Equity** grew up to fill gaps where damages were not adequate. Its remedies
  are discretionary: specific performance, injunction, rescission and
  rectification. The Judicature Acts 1873–75 merged the courts, and where common
  law and equity conflict, **equity prevails**.

**Civil law** (continental Europe, Latin America, francophone Africa):

- It has Roman law roots and is codified in comprehensive codes: the French Code
  civil of 1804 and the German BGB of 1900.
- Judges apply the code. Earlier decisions guide them but do not strictly bind.
- Trials are **inquisitorial** (the judge leads), not adversarial. Academic
  writing carries real weight.

**Sharia law** ("the path to the watering place"):

| Source or method | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Quran** | The primary source |
| **Sunnah** | The accepted practice of the Prophet |
| **Ijtihad** | Independent reasoning where the sources are silent |
| **Ijma** | Consensus of scholars |
| **Qiyas** | Reasoning by **analogy**, the nearest equivalent to precedent |
| *Taqlid* | Following established interpretation without new reasoning |
| Schools | Sunni: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali. Shia: Ja'fari |

- Commercial principles include the ban on **riba** (interest) and on
  excessive uncertainty (*gharar*). Islamic finance therefore shares profit and
  risk instead (see the partnership forms in area D).
- *Local lens:* **Kadhi's courts** in Kenya (under the Constitution) and in
  Zanzibar apply Islamic law to Muslim personal matters such as marriage,
  divorce and inheritance.

## A2. International trade, regulation and conflict of laws

### Conflict of laws (A2a)

In any cross-border dispute, ask three questions:

1. Which country's courts have **jurisdiction**?
2. Which country's **law** governs the contract?
3. Will a judgment be **recognised and enforced** in the other country?

You can reduce the risk with **choice-of-law** and **jurisdiction or
arbitration clauses**. The wider answer is international **harmonisation**.

### Tools of harmonisation (A2b)

| Instrument | Binding? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Treaty or convention | Binds states that ratify it | CISG (1980); New York Convention (1958) |
| Model law | A template that states may adopt or adapt | UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration |
| Model code, principles, legislative guide | Guidance only | UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts |
| Uniform contract terms | Bind only if the parties incorporate them | ICC Incoterms® 2020; UCP 600 |

### International organisations (A2c)

| Body | Founded | Key role |
|---|---|---|
| **United Nations** | 1945 | Peace and security, friendly relations, economic and social cooperation, human rights |
| **UNCITRAL** | 1966 | The UN's core legal body for trade law. Drafts conventions, model laws and guides; coordinates other bodies; promotes wider adoption; publishes case law (CLOUT); works with UNCTAD |
| **WTO** | 1995 (GATT 1947) | Free trade in goods, services and IP; cuts tariffs, quotas and red tape; forum for agreements; trade policy reviews; help for developing countries; state-to-state dispute settlement |
| **ICC** | 1919 | The world business organisation. Promotes trade, investment and fair competition; fights commercial crime; writes **Incoterms** and **UCP 600**; runs the **International Court of Arbitration (1923)** |
| **OECD** | 1961 (Paris) | Policy cooperation between governments; MNE Guidelines; Anti-Bribery Convention (1997); international tax work |
| **UNIDROIT** | 1926 (Rome) | Independent intergovernmental body harmonising private law. Three tiers: **Secretariat** (day to day), **Governing Council** (policy, work programme, supervises the Secretariat), **General Assembly** (ultimate decision-maker: budget, work programme, elects the Council) |

Courts: the **International Court of Justice** hears disputes between **states
only**. Regional courts such as the **East African Court of Justice (Arusha)**
interpret their treaties. Most commercial disputes go to national courts or to
arbitration.

*Local lens:* Tanzania and Kenya have been WTO members since 1995. Both belong
to the East African Community customs union, which has a common external tariff.

## A3. Alternative dispute resolution

### Court v ADR (A3a)

| Court | Arbitration |
|---|---|
| Judges are legal experts | The parties choose an expert arbitrator |
| Precedent makes outcomes predictable, and creates new law | No precedent, and less predictable |
| Rights of appeal | Very limited grounds to challenge |
| Public | Private and confidential |
| Formal, can be slow | Flexible, often quicker, though not always cheaper |

The ADR spectrum: **negotiation** (the parties alone) → **mediation** (a
neutral helps but imposes nothing) → **conciliation** (a neutral proposes
terms) → **arbitration** (a binding award) → **litigation** (a court
judgment).

### ICC International Court of Arbitration (A3b)

It **does not decide disputes itself**. It administers and supervises ICC
arbitrations: it confirms or appoints arbitrators, decides challenges,
**scrutinises draft awards**, and fixes fees and expenses.

### UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration (A3c)

- The **arbitration agreement must be in writing**. That covers a signed
  document, an exchange of letters or electronic communications, a statement of
  claim and defence that is not denied, or a contract referring to a document
  containing the clause.
- A court **must refer** the parties to arbitration unless the agreement is
  null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed.
- **Competence-competence**: the tribunal rules on its own jurisdiction.
  **Separability**: the clause survives even if the main contract is void.
- The tribunal may order **interim measures**.
- The parties must be treated **equally**, and each given a **full
  opportunity** to present its case. Otherwise the parties choose the
  procedure.

*Local lens:* Kenya's Arbitration Act 1995 is based on the Model Law.
Tanzania's Arbitration Act 2020 draws mainly on the English Arbitration Act
1996. Both countries are parties to the **New York Convention (1958)**.

### The arbitral tribunal (A3d)

- The parties choose the number of arbitrators. **If they don't, the Model Law
  says three**: each party appoints one, and those two appoint the presiding
  arbitrator. If the parties agree a sole arbitrator but not who it is, a court
  or appointing authority appoints one.
- **Nationality** alone is no bar. Arbitrators must be **impartial and
  independent**, and must **disclose** anything likely to raise justifiable
  doubts.
- **Challenge** is only for justifiable doubts as to impartiality or
  independence, or for lacking the agreed qualifications.
- If an arbitrator fails to act, they withdraw or the parties end the mandate,
  and a **substitute** is appointed.

### Arbitral awards (A3e)

- A three-member tribunal decides by **majority**. A settlement can be recorded
  as an **award on agreed terms**.
- The award is **in writing, signed, reasoned** (unless the parties agree
  otherwise), and **dated with the place** of arbitration stated.
- Clerical or calculation errors can be corrected within 30 days.
- **Setting aside (Art. 34)** is the only recourse. It must be sought from the
  court at the seat **within 3 months**, and only on these grounds:
  - incapacity of a party, or an invalid agreement
  - no proper notice, or a party unable to present its case
  - the award goes beyond the submission
  - the tribunal or procedure was not as agreed
  - the court finds the subject matter not arbitrable, or the award contrary to
    public policy
- The court does **not** review the merits. Almost the same grounds allow a
  court to refuse **recognition and enforcement** (Arts 35–36, mirroring the New
  York Convention).

## Exam traps

- **Reverse** means the same case on appeal. **Overrule** means a different,
  later case.
- **Qiyas** is analogy, the nearest thing to precedent. **Ijma** is consensus.
- A **model law** binds no one until a state enacts it. A **convention** binds
  ratifying states.
- The ICC Court **supervises**. It does not decide disputes.
- The default number of arbitrators is **three**, not one.
- A wrong decision on the merits is **not** a ground for setting aside.

## Practice questions

1. Which body is UNIDROIT's ultimate decision-maker? *(General Assembly)*
2. A party learns the award contains a calculation error. What can it ask for,
   and within what time? *(Correction by the tribunal, within 30 days)*
3. Name the four conditions for a precedent to bind. *(Point of law; ratio;
   material facts similar; higher court)*
4. Under the Model Law, how many months does a party have to apply to set aside
   an award? *(3)*
