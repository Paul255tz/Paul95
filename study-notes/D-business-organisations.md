# D. Formation and constitution of business organisations

**Episodes 13–17** · Syllabus D1 (agency), D2 (partnerships), D3 (corporations
and legal personality), D4 (formation and constitution of a company). Statutes
are the UK model used by ACCA: the Partnership Act 1890, Limited Partnerships
Act 1907, Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000 and Companies Act 2006.

## D1. Agency law

**The role of an agent:** to bring the **principal** into a legal relationship
with a **third party**. The contract is between the principal and the third
party, and the agent usually drops out. Key examples:

- **Partners** are agents of the firm and of each other (PA 1890 s5).
- **Directors** act as agents of the company.
- Others include employees, brokers, estate agents and commercial agents.

**Creating agency:**

| Method | Point to remember |
|---|---|
| Express agreement | Oral or written. A power of attorney must be made formally (by deed) |
| Implied agreement | From conduct or the relationship |
| **Ratification** | The principal later adopts an unauthorised contract, and it works **retrospectively**. Conditions: the agent claimed to act for that principal; the principal **existed** and had **capacity** when the contract was made (*Kelner v Baxter*, 1866); the whole contract is ratified within a reasonable time |
| Estoppel / holding out | The principal represents that someone is its agent and cannot later deny it |
| Necessity | A genuine emergency, the owner cannot be contacted, and the agent acts in good faith for the owner's benefit |

**Authority:**

- **Express actual:** what the principal actually authorised.
- **Implied actual:** what is incidental to that, or usual for that position,
  unless forbidden. *Hely-Hutchinson v Brayhead* (1968): a chairman acting as
  chief executive had implied authority.
- **Apparent (ostensible):** what the principal led the third party to believe.
  It needs a **representation by the principal** (not the agent), **reliance**
  on it, and the third party **acting** on it. *Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst
  Park Properties* (1964): a director acting as managing director with the
  board's knowledge bound the company.

**Liability:**

- Within actual or apparent authority, the **principal** is liable. The agent
  generally cannot sue or be sued on the contract.
- The **agent** is personally liable:
  - for **breach of warranty of authority** (*Collen v Wright*, 1857)
  - where the principal is **undisclosed** (the third party may sue either,
    once it discovers the principal)
  - where the principal **doesn't exist** (pre-incorporation contracts)
  - where trade custom or the form of the contract makes it liable
- **Agent's duties** include performance, obedience, care and skill, personal
  performance, accounting, avoiding conflicts, and taking **no secret profits
  or bribes**.

**Ending an agency:** by agreement, by revocation, or automatically on death,
insanity, or the principal's bankruptcy. Tell third parties, or apparent
authority may continue.

## D2. Partnerships

**Definition (PA 1890 s1):** "the relation which subsists between persons
carrying on a business in common with a view of profit". No formality is
needed. Sharing **profits** is strong evidence of a partnership. Sharing
**gross returns** is not. In England a partnership has **no separate legal
personality**. The old 20-partner limit was abolished in 2002.

**Default rules** (PA 1890 ss24–30; the notes' mnemonic is "medics in bed"),
which apply unless the partners agree otherwise:

- capital and profits are shared **equally**, and so are losses
- no **interest on capital**, but **5%** on advances beyond agreed capital
- every partner may **manage**, and there is **no salary**
- partners are **indemnified** for liabilities properly incurred for the firm
- ordinary matters are decided by **majority**, but changing the nature of the
  business or **admitting a partner** needs **unanimity**
- the **books** are open to every partner, and there is **no expulsion** unless
  the agreement allows it
- **secret profits** and profits of a **competing business** must be handed to
  the firm

**Partners' authority (s5):** every partner binds the firm for business of the
kind the firm carries on, in the usual way. The firm is not bound if the partner
lacked authority **and** the third party knew that, or didn't know or believe
him to be a partner. Private use of the firm's credit doesn't bind the firm
(s7). Someone **held out** as a partner is liable as one (s14).

**Liability:**

- **Unlimited**: joint for debts (s9), joint and several for wrongs (ss10–12).
- An **incoming** partner is not liable for earlier debts.
- A **retiring** partner stays liable for debts incurred while a partner. It is
  also liable for **later** debts to people who didn't know of the retirement,
  unless notice was given: **actual notice** to existing customers and a
  **Gazette** notice for others (s36).
- **Novation** can release a retiring partner from old debts.

**Dissolution:**

- **Automatic** (unless agreed otherwise): the fixed term expires; the single
  venture is complete; a partner gives notice (partnership at will); a partner
  dies or becomes **bankrupt**; the business becomes **illegal**. If a partner's
  share is **charged** for a private debt, the other partners may choose to
  dissolve.
- **By the court** (s35): permanent incapacity; conduct prejudicial to the
  business; wilful or persistent breach; the business can only run at a loss;
  just and equitable.

**After dissolution (s44):** assets are paid out in this order:

1. outside creditors
2. partners' **loans and advances**
3. partners' **capital**
4. the surplus, in the profit-sharing ratio

Losses are met from profits, then capital, then the partners personally. Under
*Garner v Murray* (1904), a deficiency left by an insolvent partner is borne by
the solvent partners in proportion to their **capital**.

| | General partnership | Limited partnership (1907 Act) | LLP (2000 Act) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal person? | No (England) | No | **Yes** |
| Liability | Unlimited | At least one **general** partner unlimited; **limited** partners liable up to their capital | Members' liability limited |
| Registration | None | Must register | Must register; file accounts; audit unless exempt |
| Management | All partners | A limited partner who manages **loses** limited liability, and cannot withdraw capital | By members' agreement |
| Insolvency | — | — | Fraudulent and wrongful trading rules apply |

**Abroad:**

- **France:** the *société en nom collectif* (SNC) is like a general
  partnership; the *société en commandite simple* (SCS) is like a limited
  partnership.
- **Islamic law:**
  - **Musharakah**: partners pool capital and share profit, loss and decisions.
  - **Mudarabah**: one provides capital, the other manages, and profit is
    shared instead of interest.
  - **Shirkah al-inan**: partners invest and share profits, but are not liable
    for each other's debts.
- *Local lens:* Kenya has a Partnerships Act (2012) and a Limited Liability
  Partnerships Act (2011).

## D3. Corporations and legal personality

| | Sole trader | Partnership | Company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal personality | None | None (England) | **Separate** |
| Liability | Unlimited | Usually unlimited | Members usually **limited** |
| Formality and publicity | Minimal | Minimal | Registration, filings, public records |
| Continuity | Ends with the owner | Dissolves on change | **Perpetual succession** |
| Raising finance | Limited | Cannot generally grant a **floating charge** | Shares; floating charges |
| Property | Owned by the trader | Partners co-own | Owned by the **company** |

- A **corporation sole** is a single office holder (a mayor or bishop). A
  **corporation aggregate** is many members forming one legal person.
- Corporations are created by **royal charter**, by **statute**, or, most
  commonly, by **registration**.
- **Limited liability:** members' liability is limited to the unpaid amount on
  their shares, or to their guarantee. The **company** stays fully liable for
  its own debts. Companies may be limited by shares, limited by guarantee, or
  unlimited. A **community interest company** (CIC) is a further form.

**Public v private:**

- A **public company (plc)** must:
  - be limited by shares, say in its constitution that it is public, and end its
    name with "plc"
  - have **allotted share capital of at least £50,000**, with at least **¼ paid
    up** plus the whole premium
  - obtain a **trading certificate** before it trades or borrows
  - have at least **2 directors** and a **qualified secretary**
- Only a plc may **offer shares to the public**.
- A **private** company needs 1 director, no secretary, and has no minimum
  capital.
- *Local lens:* in **Kenya and Tanzania**, a private company is limited to
  **50 members**.
- **Abroad:** France has the **SA** (like a public company) and the **SARL**
  (like a private one, with capital in parts that cannot be offered to the
  public). Germany has the **AG** and the **GmbH**. The EU has the **SE**
  (European public company, minimum capital €120,000). Islamic law has no
  separate corporate form.

**Separate personality:**

- *Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd* (1897): a validly formed company is a person
  distinct from its members. Mr Salomon, as a secured debenture holder, was
  paid ahead of the unsecured creditors.
- *Macaura v Northern Assurance* (1925): a shareholder had no insurable interest
  in the company's timber.
- *Lee v Lee's Air Farming* (1961): a controlling director could also be the
  company's employee.

**Lifting the veil (the exception):**

- **Sham or façade to evade an existing obligation:** *Gilford Motor Co v Horne*
  (1933), a non-compete covenant; *Jones v Lipman* (1962), a sale of land. *Prest
  v Petrodel* (2013) confirmed the narrow **evasion principle**.
- **Enemy character in wartime:** *Daimler v Continental Tyre* (1916).
- **Groups:** generally **not** lifted (*Adams v Cape Industries*, 1990).
- **Statute:**
  - fraudulent and wrongful trading (IA 1986 ss213–214)
  - a plc trading without a certificate (CA 2006 s767)
  - reusing a liquidated company's name (IA s216)
  - acting while disqualified (CDDA s15)
  - group accounts

## D4. Formation and constitution of a company

**Promoters:**

- A promoter is one who "undertakes to form a company … and to set it going"
  (*Twycross v Grant*, 1877). Professionals acting only in a professional
  capacity are not promoters.
- Promoters owe **fiduciary duties**: no secret profit, and **disclosure** of
  any interest to an independent board or to the members.
- Remedies for breach:
  - **rescission** (*Erlanger v New Sombrero Phosphate*, 1878)
  - recovery of **secret profit** (*Gluckstein v Barnes*, 1900)
  - **damages** (*Re Leeds & Hanley Theatres*, 1902)

**Pre-incorporation contracts:**

- A company cannot ratify a contract made before it existed (*Kelner v Baxter*).
- Under **CA 2006 s51**, the contract takes effect with the **person acting**,
  who is **personally liable** unless the contract says otherwise (*Phonogram v
  Lane*, 1982).
- To avoid liability: buy an **off-the-shelf** company; sign only a **draft**; or,
  after incorporation, the company makes a new contract (**novation**).

**Registration (CA 2006 ss7–16):**

- The **memorandum of association**: the subscribers wish to form a company and
  each take at least one share.
- The **application** for registration: proposed name; registered office
  location; limited by shares or guarantee; private or public. It comes with:
  - a **statement of capital** and initial shareholdings
  - a statement of **proposed officers**
  - a statement of initial **significant control**
  - **articles** (unless model articles apply)
  - a **statement of compliance**
  - the fee
- The **certificate of incorporation** is **conclusive evidence** of
  incorporation (*Jubilee Cotton Mills v Lewis*, 1924).
- **Streamlined registration:** private companies can be formed online, often
  within a day, with tax registration done at the same time.
- *Local lens:* in Tanzania, registration is through **BRELA's Online
  Registration System**, including **beneficial ownership** filing. In Kenya, it
  is through the **Business Registration Service** on eCitizen.

**Statutory books and returns:**

- Registers of **members**, **directors** (and their residential addresses),
  **secretaries**, **people with significant control**, and copies of
  **charge** instruments.
- **Minutes** (kept 10 years) and **accounting records**.
- Private companies may elect to keep certain information on the **central
  register** instead.
- Every year the company files its **accounts** and a **confirmation
  statement**, which replaced the annual return in 2016.
- **Persons with significant control (PSC):**
  - more than **25% of the shares**
  - more than **25% of the votes**
  - the right to appoint or remove a **majority of the board**
  - otherwise significant **influence or control**, including through a trust
    or firm
- From **18 November 2025**, new UK directors and PSCs must **verify their
  identity** with Companies House (ECCTA 2023).

**The constitution:**

- The constitution is the **articles** plus resolutions and agreements
  affecting them (s17). The memorandum is now only historical. **Objects are
  unrestricted** unless the articles restrict them (s31).
- **The articles as a statutory contract (s33):**
  - member → company (*Hickman v Kent or Romney Marsh Sheep-Breeders*, 1915)
  - company → member (*Pender v Lushington*, 1877)
  - member → member (*Rayfield v Hands*, 1960)
  - **not** outsiders, or members acting in another capacity (*Eley v Positive
    Government Security Life Assurance*, 1876)
- **Model articles** (Companies (Model Articles) Regulations 2008) apply by
  default:
  - directors manage, but members can direct them by **special resolution**
  - directors are appointed by **ordinary resolution or by the board**
  - dividends are declared by **ordinary resolution**, never above the
    directors' recommendation
  - they also cover meetings, voting, polls and proxies, and administrative
    matters
- **Changing the articles:**
  - by **special resolution** (75%), with a copy filed within 15 days
  - provisions may be **entrenched** with extra conditions, but can never be
    made unalterable
  - no member can be made to take more shares or pay more without written
    consent
  - the change must be **bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole**:
    *Allen v Gold Reefs* (1900); *Greenhalgh v Arderne Cinemas* (1951, the
    hypothetical member test); *Sidebottom v Kershaw Leese* (1920, expelling a
    competitor was valid); *Brown v British Abrasive Wheel* (1919, invalid)
  - a valid change can still breach an outside contract (*Southern Foundries v
    Shirlaw*, 1940)
- **Capacity:** a company's capacity can't be questioned because of its
  constitution (s39). For a **third party in good faith**, the directors' power
  to bind the company is free of constitutional limits (s40). Good faith is
  presumed. Members can still restrain an act beyond the objects before it
  happens.
- **Names (ss53–81):**
  - must end with **Ltd or plc**; cannot be the **same** as an existing name;
    nothing **offensive**; **sensitive words** need approval
  - the Secretary of State can direct a change within **12 months** (too like an
    existing name) or **5 years** (misleading information given), or at any time
    if the name misleads the public about the company's activities
  - complaints go to the **company names adjudicator**, and passing off
    (*Ewing v Buttercup Margarine*, 1917) is also available
  - to change its name, a company passes a **special resolution** and receives a
    new certificate

## Exam traps

- Apparent authority needs a representation by the **principal**.
- In a pre-incorporation contract, the **person signing** is liable.
- The articles **don't** bind the company to outsiders.
- A plc needs **£50,000 allotted** capital, **¼ paid up**.
- A PSC holds **more than 25%**, not 25% exactly.
